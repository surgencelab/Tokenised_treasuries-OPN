Tokenized treasuries began as a niche experiment and became where on-chain capital parks its cash.

In 2021, Franklin Templeton put a US government money fund on a public blockchain, BlackRock's BUIDL gave the category more institutional credibility on its 2024 launch, and the market has since grown past $10B across sixteen chains and seven issuers.

Usually, coverage of this market stops at Ethereum but this report is built on data indexed directly from every chain these products live on, alongside secondary data sets. And one of the trends that have stood out in our findings is that markets with real institutional demand are concentrated into a handful of products, issuers, and in some cases single wallets, and yet it is still short of the infrastructure needed to scale.

TL;DR

  • According to CoinDesk, Circle's USYC is now the largest single product at $3.00B, ahead of BlackRock's BUIDL family ($2.61B) and Ondo's USDY/OUSG ($2.56B). This was also verified on-chain.

  • The holder base is small and concentrated. Across 27 deployments, 16 have fewer than ten holders. Analysts flag the same concentration and question how much of the market is active at all.

1. The cash sleeve goes on-chain

So what are we actually talking about? A tokenized treasury is just a fund share issued as a token, backed by short-dated US Treasury bills, government money-market holdings, or overnight repo. It pays the underlying yield, redeems back to a stablecoin or fiat, and trades around the clock. Somewhere between the first fund in 2022 and now, it became the default place to park on-chain cash that still earns.

The largest asset manager in the world frames the direction plainly:

"Every stock, every bond, every fund, every asset can be tokenized."[1] Larry Fink, Chairman & CEO, BlackRock (2025 Annual Chairman's Letter to Investors)

Fink even put a number on it, sketching a path to tokenize the roughly $110T of global stocks and bonds and calling this "the beginning of the tokenization of all assets."[2] Tokenized treasuries are the piece of that vision already working at scale, which makes them the best place to see what on-chain finance actually needs from its infrastructure.

Four things decide whether one of these tokens works: issuance that can control who's allowed to hold it, a price feed that reports the fund's real value, settlement that clears fast and for good, and reach across chains that doesn't split the liquidity in two. Those four are the rails the whole market runs on, and getting all four into one place is what OPN Chain is trying to do.

2. A $10.5B market, and how fast it arrived

That scale is the only reason the rails matter, so start there. The tracked market sits at $10.52B, which is 96.1% of the $10.95B rwa.xyz reports for the same products, checked deployment by deployment. Zoom out and it's part of something bigger: the wider tokenized-treasury market crossed $11B in early 2026,[3] and independent trackers put all tokenized real-world assets near $60B.[4] Treasuries are the biggest liquid slice of that.

And it's recent. The climb is really the interesting part, running from a single Franklin fund on Stellar in early 2022 to today's crowded field, with the steepest stretch through 2024 and 2025 as BlackRock, Ondo, Circle, and Superstate scaled across chains.

A base compounding this fast, across this many networks, is exactly what turns "good enough" infrastructure into a real constraint, and it's why the question of what these assets settle on is starting to matter as much as the assets themselves. One caveat before you read the charts: the current-state numbers (total AUM, market share, chain split, holders) cover every chain, while the history and flow charts are labeled EVM-only, because we hold the non-EVM chains as verified current snapshots while we backfill their full event history.

3. The new order: Circle out front, BNB Chain in second

A market this big begs the obvious question: who owns it? The league table has flipped at the top. USYC, Circle's yield-bearing token from the Hashnote deal, is now the single biggest product at $3.00B, a 28.6% share. USDY is next at $2.15B (20.5%), then BUIDL at $1.65B (15.7%), with its institutional class BUIDL-I adding another $962M (9.2%). After that it's WisdomTree's WTGXX at $752M, Franklin's BENJI at $734M, Superstate's USTB at $677M, Ondo's OUSG at $405M, and OpenEden's TBILL at $183M.

Group it by issuer and the top turns into a three-way race:

In March 2026, CoinDesk reported that "Circle overtakes BlackRock in tokenized treasuries as market hits $11 billion."[3] Circle's CEO tied the move to institutional demand when it bought Hashnote:

"The integration of USYC and Hashnote into Circle's platform marks a major moment… as cash and yield-bearing short-duration treasury bill assets become fungible and convertible at the speed of blockchains and crypto capital markets. This is a huge unlock for a market that is increasingly being driven by institutional adoption."[5] Jeremy Allaire, Co-Founder, Chairman & CEO, Circle

The top three now hold roughly $2.5B to $3.0B each, all within $450M of one another. However you cut it, the concentration is stark: our July data puts those three (Circle, BlackRock, Ondo) at close to 78% of tracked AUM, with Circle having bumped Franklin off the top spot. Forbes found the same shape across the whole tokenized market, where the top five assets hold about half of everything.[4]

The chain split is where this insight gets exciting. When you map the AUM across every network these products live on you can see where the market actually sits:

BNB Chain holds $3.17B of tokenized treasuries, second only to Ethereum, and USYC is the reason: $2.91B of it lives on BNB, with TBILL ($151M) and BUIDL ($111M) making up the rest. USYC on Ethereum is only $91.6M, so 97% of Circle's flagship token sits on BNB. Hashnote launched it there in November 2025, and Circle's own disclosures show the BNB supply ballooning once Binance began taking it as off-exchange collateral.[6]

That reach isn't free though. USDY runs on eleven chains, BUIDL and BENJI on eight apiece, and the market's AUM is scattered across sixteen networks that don't natively talk to each other. Every new deployment splinters an issuer's own liquidity, holders, and reporting, then stitches them back together with bridges and a fresh compliance layer on each chain. The market has clearly voted for going multichain; what it doesn't have yet is a settlement layer where that reach doesn't cost it fragmentation, and that is exactly the gap a sovereign, interoperable chain like OPN Chain is built to fill.

4. The flows: BlackRock scaling, rivals redeeming

Usually, money flows tell you which way things are heading. On the EVM deployments, the last 30 days netted +$87.2M, and the mix matters more than the total.

BlackRock alone added $372.3M of BUIDL and $132.6M of BUIDL-I, roughly half a billion in fresh institutional subscriptions. Over the same stretch, USTB (-$159.8M), USYC-EVM (-$79.9M), BENJI (-$78.2M), and OUSG (-$74.9M) all saw net redemptions, while USDY barely moved.

So the momentum is really one issuer's story. BlackRock is scaling while several rivals hand capital back, which is what you'd expect in a market where allocators pile into the biggest, most liquid, best-backed names. Moody's handing BUIDL a Aaa-mf rating in May 2026, its top grade for a tokenized money fund, is part of that pull.[7] In a market consolidating this hard, the edge goes to whoever offers the most credible, compliant home for the assets, which is as much an infrastructure question as a fund-management one.

5. Held by the few: tokenised asset holders

Across 27 deployments there are about 5,453 holder positions, and 16 of those 27 have fewer than ten holders each.

At the top, the concentration is extreme. USYC on BNB Chain, the single biggest position anywhere at $2.91B, is held by five wallets, and one of them holds basically all of it (2.574B tokens, an HHI of 1.0). Line up the largest single positions and they read like a roll call of institutional balance sheets: USYC-BNB at $2.9B, BUIDL-I at 715M tokens, WTGXX at 550M, BUIDL on Avalanche at 500M, USDY on Ethereum at 359M.

Before anyone reads that one wallet as a single point of failure, there's a documented reason for it. Binance takes USYC as yield-bearing off-exchange collateral for its institutional clients,[6] so what looks like one giant holder is really an exchange custody-and-collateral structure. It reflects exchange plumbing rather than one investor's bet.

A few products are more spread out. USDY on Ethereum has 918 holders with a median position around $10, and BENJI on Stellar has 991. But those are the exceptions. The typical deployment is a handful of wallets moving institutional-size tickets, and the holders are funds, market makers, exchanges, and stablecoin issuers. The wallet structure gives it away.

Zoom out and independent surveys land in the same place. A July 2026 Forbes analysis of the $60B tokenized-asset market found $32.9B across 910 assets with zero weekly transfers, and pointed out that a lot of that supply was never meant to move in public.[4] That's the same concentration and inactivity our wallet data shows, just at market scale.

None of this is an accident of how the tokens are built. When a market is owned by a small set of known, whitelisted institutions, it's governed by eligibility rules, not open transfer, and BlackRock already runs one compliance and whitelist layer across all of BUIDL's chains so only approved wallets can touch it. As these products spread onto more networks, enforcing that eligibility at the token level, tied to a real identity, stops being a detail and becomes the whole engineering problem, and it is one OPN Chain's compliant issuance and NeoID identity layer are built to solve at the base layer.

6. Where the yield lives, and why NAV matters

That concentrated institutional base is here for one thing above all: the return. Yield shows up two ways in the data. Peg products (BUIDL, BENJI, WTGXX) hold a $1 price and pay out as fresh tokens. Accruing products carry a rising per-token NAV instead: OUSG at $115.89, USTB at $11.14, TBILL at $1.150, USDY at $1.139, USYC at $1.131 as of 15 July. Those NAVs are just cumulative return baked in since launch.

The rates themselves hug the front end of the Treasury curve. Ondo reported USDY paying 4.65% APY in April 2026, right around the 3-month T-bill less fees.[8] We benchmark realized NAV growth against that 3-month bill (FRED) rather than quoting one headline APY, since pass-through and fees vary by product.

Here's why the price feed is the load-bearing piece of the whole thing. Every AUM number for an accruing fund is only as good as its NAV source. OUSG is the proof: the same on-chain supply is worth $183M at its real NAV and $1.6M if you naively peg it at $1. A market this size can't run on rough valuations, which makes a reliable on-chain NAV or oracle a precondition, not a nice-to-have, and it's one of the four things OPN Chain aims to provide natively rather than leave to each issuer.

7. Yield: Working capital

Yield explains why institutions hold these tokens. The next question is what they actually do with them. The whole point over a bank money fund is that these can move, settle, and post as collateral on-chain. You see it most clearly on BNB Chain, where Circle disclosed Binance taking USYC as yield-bearing off-exchange collateral,[6] and in the stablecoin plumbing where instant-redeem products back on-chain dollars.

The other side of the story deserves equal airtime. That same Forbes survey argues most tokenized value just sits there, and one builder put it bluntly:

"A $60 billion market that 97% of people can't touch, where half the assets never move, isn't a market yet. It's a waiting room." David Taylor, Co-Founder & CEO, EtherFuse (via Forbes, July 2026)

Our data lands in the middle. A concentrated set of tokens really is used as collateral and settlement, while the long tail does nothing. Whether that active core widens out is the open question for the category, and widening it depends less on more demand than on rails that let these tokens move, settle, and post as collateral as easily as a stablecoin does.[4]

8. Getting out: redemption sorts the market

Posting a token as collateral only works if you can get out of it fast, so redemption is what really sorts the market. BUIDL, USYC, and OUSG offer same-day redemption to a stablecoin through dedicated facilities, which is exactly why they show up as collateral inside stablecoin and exchange systems. BENJI, WTGXX, and USTB run on a slower T+1 fund cycle. USDY leans on issuer redemption plus secondary AMM liquidity, which can slip under stress.

The pattern holds right across the board. The products winning distribution are the ones whose settlement is fast, final, and wired straight into stablecoin and exchange systems, because redemption speed is really just a property of the settlement layer underneath. It tracks almost one-to-one with which tokens became collateral: quick to exit, and the token gets used; slow to exit, and it sits parked. Settlement that is fast and final by default, rather than bolted on per venue, is one of the things a purpose-built chain like OPN Chain is meant to guarantee.

9. The wrapper decides who can hold

Redemption happens inside a legal wrapper, and that wrapper also sets who's even allowed to hold the token. USTB is a 3(c)(7) fund limited to qualified purchasers, BENJI is a 1940-Act registered money fund, and USDY is a Reg S note for non-US buyers.

The part that makes them work on-chain is that the contract enforces those rules itself. Every product in the set restricts who can hold or receive it, so effectively 100% of the $10.5B we track is permissioned supply:

The IMF, in its April 2026 note on tokenized finance, warns that these things straddle unsettled legal and jurisdictional lines, with resolution powers still stuck at the national level even as the tokens move across borders.[9] Analysts keep flagging the related risk that some of these get classed as unregistered securities. The through-line is simple: these structures only hold up on-chain if the token can enforce eligibility at the moment of transfer, which is where compliant issuance meets verifiable identity, and where a chain with identity as a native primitive has a structural edge.

10. Where the risk concentrates

Those structures set the rules; the data shows where the exposure actually sits. Concentration is the headline risk, and outside sources back it up: our data puts the top three issuers near 78% of tracked AUM, and Forbes found the top five tokenized assets holding about half the entire market.[4] When single wallets hold billions and 16 of 27 deployments have fewer than ten holders, the whole thing rides on the decisions of a few big players.

Activity is the second worry. If a big chunk of tokenized value never moves, as Forbes argues, then headline AUM is flattering the real economic footprint. NAV and de-peg risk sit with the accruing and AMM-exit products when markets get rough, and regulatory and counterparty risk runs through every issuer, custodian, and transfer agent. On the other side of the ledger, signals like Moody's Aaa-mf on BUIDL show the top of the market maturing even while the tail stays thin.[7] Most of these are really infrastructure risks in disguise, the kind a chain built for compliant, well-priced, verifiable assets is meant to contain.

11. The rails the market is still missing

All of those risks trace back to one gap in the plumbing. Put the findings together and you get a market with real product-market fit and deep institutional demand that's still building its infrastructure by hand: eligibility enforced through a different whitelist on each of sixteen chains, valuations only as good as each issuer's NAV feed, distribution decided by who can redeem to a stablecoin fastest, and liquidity re-split with every new deployment. Each of these is solved on its own and unsolved together.

That's the gap OPN Chain is built to close, and the mapping comes straight off the data. The 100%-permissioned supply and tiny, whitelisted holder sets argue for issuance and identity as a base-layer primitive instead of a per-chain add-on, which is exactly what OPN Chain's compliant tokenization rails and NeoID identity layer are meant to be. The OUSG blow-up, where a wrong feed misreports a fund by two orders of magnitude, argues for a native on-chain NAV and oracle layer. The dominance of same-day-redemption products argues for high-throughput, sub-second settlement. And $10.5B scattered across sixteen networks argues for a sovereign, interoperable L1 instead of a pile of bridges.

IOPn's co-founder and COO, Jimi Ibrahim, talks about the infrastructure itself as the trust layer, describing an identity and agent design that treats adversarial cases as "a first principle, not an afterthought" and insists on "human-in-the-loop authorization for consequential actions." At the May 2026 launch of the UAE's first on-chain business-identity system on OPN Chain, he called the UAE one of the most "institutionally stable jurisdictions" for regulated issuance.[10]

The demand is settled: north of $10B and still climbing. What's missing is a single venue where compliant issuance, reliable NAV, fast settlement, and native cross-chain reach come from the chain itself, instead of four separate integrations bolted on after the fact.

Methodology and coverage

Supply is rebuilt from on-chain mint and redemption events on EVM and verified against totalSupply(); non-EVM chains (Stellar, Solana, Aptos, Sui, Noble, XRP Ledger, Sei, Plume) use verified snapshots from each chain's public API with reconstructed daily series. AUM = supply × NAV; peg products at $1, accruing products at the dated NAVs in the data pack.

In total this covers $10.52B, or 96.1% of the $10.95B rwa.xyz reports for the same products, reconciled on 15 July 2026. Coverage is complete or near-complete for every product except three, which we name rather than paper over: BUIDL (91.1%, ~$256M unaccounted after adding BNB Chain), OUSG (84.4%, ~$75M), and TBILL (85.8%, with a ~$30M XRP Ledger slice held back until its issuer address is confirmed). Every non-EVM deployment is read from its own chain's API and checked against on-chain supply; BENJI's Stellar issuer, for one, was identified among 17 lookalikes by its 1,323 authorized accounts.
